## Abstract

In precision manipulation, small errors in alignment or force can cause jamming or deformation, making contact feedback essential for adapting pretrained vision–language–action (VLA) models to these tasks. A single tactile observation leaves the response to recent actions ambiguous, and directly introducing tactile inputs during post-training can disrupt general-purpose capabilities acquired during pretraining and draw visual attention away from contact regions. To address these challenges, CoRA-VTLA augments pretrained VLAs with lightweight tactile modules. A queryable action–response memory retrieves recent contact responses so that the action expert can combine them with immediate tactile feedback. Contact-guided gaze supervision concentrates the visual attention of the tactile semantic token on image regions corresponding to fingertip contact. CoRA-VTLA achieves 73.33% mean success across three MuJoCo tasks, compared with 50% for direct tactile fusion with the same sensor inputs. It also achieves 67% success on an optical-tactile task from the public UniVTAC benchmark and 66.7% mean success across three real-robot tasks.

## Method

<Wide>
  <div class="mx-auto max-w-5xl">
  <Figure>
    <img slot="figure" src="/images/Fig2-VTLA.svg" alt="CoRA-VTLA architecture with the semantic branch, contact token, interaction memory token, and response pretraining."  class="w-full h-auto" />
    <Fragment slot="caption">
      CoRA-VTLA adds three lightweight tactile pathways to a pretrained π<sub>0.5</sub> policy: a <b>current contact token</b> carrying the instantaneous pressure and wrist force/torque, a <b>queryable action–response memory token</b> that encodes the last 0.8&nbsp;s of measured contact responses and is read with a query built from the current contact and robot state, and a <b>tactile semantic token</b> appended to the VLM prefix, grounded by contact-guided gaze supervision.
    </Fragment>
  </Figure>
  </div>
</Wide>

The two action-side encoders are first initialized by **response pretraining**: from task demonstrations alone, they are trained to predict the next-step change in pressure, wrist force/torque, and robot motion, after which the temporary prediction head is discarded. All modules are then trained jointly with the full policy under the standard flow-matching objective, with attribute and gaze supervision on the semantic branch.
